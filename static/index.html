    What you get: one row per episode — show, title, date, length, encore flag, tags and re-air dates, plus links to the episode page, the original transcript, our transcript, and the audio.
    Not included: transcript text itself. A single episode runs to ~49,000 characters and a spreadsheet cell tops out at 50,000 — the links keep it readable instead of silently cut off.

    What's in the file, exactly

    Where it comes from. These rows are read from this archive — built from your two feeds and refreshed daily — not fetched from Podbean when you press the button. That is why it is instant, and why the audio and artwork are links rather than files: Podbean is still hosting those, and a spreadsheet cell cannot hold 27 MB of MP3.

    Results & cataloguing

    Jumping to a moment

    When your words were spoken aloud, the timestamps under a result show exactly where. Click one and the episode starts playing from that second — no scrubbing.

    Show notes

    Descriptions are shortened to three lines. More at the end of the last line opens the rest — the notes as published, including anything the feed carries that doesn't fit. When you've searched, the blurb shows the matching passage instead, and Show full notes opens the whole thing underneath it.

    Tags

    The names under each episode are people, bands, museums and books the producers linked in the show notes — a hand-curated index, not guesswork. Click one to see every episode tagged with it.

    Topics

    Show notes say what happened, not what an episode is about. The gold-edged topics are read from the notes and the transcript, so they exist even where nobody wrote them down. Click one to see everything on that subject. Unlike Tags, these are inferred — right nearly always, but worth a glance before you cite them.

    Guests and interviewers

    Who was on, and who was asking. Read from the same pass, which catches the guests who were never hyperlinked. Click any name to see every episode they appear in — the producers' links and the ones found in the audio both count.

    Also ran

    A gold line under an episode means the same programme aired more than once — as an encore, or on both shows. That's the quick answer to "have we run this already?"

    Transcript

    Transcript beside the episode title opens the words with the tools around them, already marked up with whatever you searched for. Two gestures do most of the work: click a line to hear it, and drag across the text to select a passage.

    • Click a line — or its timestamp — to play from there. Tick Follow audio and the transcript scrolls along as it goes, keeping the spoken line in view.
    • Drag across the text to select a passage. The bar along the bottom reports exactly how long it runs, where it starts and ends, and its out-cue — the last few words, so the studio knows when to come back. From there, Edit audio opens it in the clip editor and Copy passage takes the words.
    • Edit on a single line does the same for just that line — always showing on lines that matched your search, so a hit is one click from a waveform.
    • Find within the episode using the same shortcuts as the main search ("phrases", AND/OR/NOT, organiz*), with a hit count and Enter / Shift+Enter to step through them.
    • Matches only collapses a 55-minute show down to just the lines that mention the thing you're after — the fastest way from a word to the moment it was said.
    • Print it, or download it as text, or as subtitles (SRT / VTT) for a video editor.

    144 of the 200 episodes have one. The rest say so plainly rather than pretending to be broken.

    Clips & audio editing

    Making a clip

    Click a timestamp to start playing, then press the scissors at the right-hand end of the player. That opens the audio editor on what you're listening to — the passage you clicked if you're still inside it, otherwise wherever you've scrubbed to.

    The clip is copied straight out of the original file without re-encoding, so it is identical in quality to the broadcast, not a second-generation copy. Cuts land within a fortieth of a second of the mark.

    The audio editor

    Two waveforms, each with its own timeline. The top one is the whole episode; the bottom one is the selection close up, and it's where the work gets done.

    • Click the top waveform to listen from that point — for finding a moment when you don't yet know where it is. Drag it instead and the selection moves there, keeping its length.
    • Drag the red handles to set the in and out points.
    • Zoom with the − and + buttons, or the scroll wheel over the lower waveform. The readout shows how much time you're looking at. While the whole selection fits it stays centred; zoom in closer than that and the view follows whichever handle you touched last, so you can work right down to a fraction of a second on one edge. ⤢ puts the whole selection back in view.
    • The lower waveform is drawn in detail — solid in the middle for how loud a moment actually is, outlined for how far it peaked. The two faint horizontal lines mark this episode's own silence: gaps that dip inside them are the pauses between words, and they're where Snap will put a cut.
    • Arrow keys nudge a selected handle by 0.1s — hold Shift for a full second. Dragging can't reliably land on a word boundary; nudging can.
    • Mark as you listen. Play, and when you hear the place a clip should begin press I; when you hear where it should end press O. The mark lands on the playhead, so you set a point by ear rather than by dragging to a spot you've already heard go past.
    • Jump between pauses with [ and ] — the playhead moves to the middle of the next gap between words, backwards or forwards. Sentences end in silence, so this is usually the fastest way to reach the edge you want before pressing I or O.

    Hearing the edit before you commit

    An edit is judged by ear, not by eye, so there are several ways to listen. The transport sits between the two waveforms:

    • Play — exactly what will be downloaded. Press it again to pause where you are; Space does the same thing from anywhere in the editor.
    • Stop returns to the in-point, where pause holds its place — so you can stop, look, and carry on from the same spot. Home also stops.
    • Repeat plays the selection over and over until you stop it, and it follows the handles as you move them — so you can drag an edge and hear the new version come round, rather than adjusting, pressing play, listening, and adjusting again.
    • Audition in and Audition out — two seconds either side of a single cut point, so you hear the approach to an edit and what follows it. That's how you tell a cut is in the right place, and it's why they're separate: an edit is judged one edge at a time.

    Snap to silence

    Moves a cut point to the nearest gap between words. A cut through the middle of a syllable sounds broken; one that lands in the pause sounds deliberate. It only searches a second and a half either way, so it tidies a mark rather than moving it somewhere else.

    It needs the waveform, so it stays greyed out until the audio has finished loading.

    What you get

    An MP3 named for where it came from — show, date, title and timecode, like LHPH_2026-02-12_Striking-At-Kings_4330-4353.mp3 — so a clip sitting in a folder in six months still explains itself. The same details are written into the file's tags.

    The download button shows the size before you commit to it. All of this happens in your browser; the audio comes straight from the podcast host.

    Your clips

    Keeping a clip

    Finding a moment in 143 hours is work worth doing once. In the editor, ＋ Add to library opens a short dialogue where you name the clip and give it labels, then saves it — without closing the editor, so you can nudge a handle and save a second version of the same quote.

    Clips in the top bar opens the list, with the count beside it.

    Labels

    Your own words — promo, intro, out-cue, whatever suits how you work. A clip can carry any number, and the most-used ones sit across the top of the list as filters. Choosing two shows only clips carrying both.

    Typing promo when Promo already exists reuses the one you have rather than making a second. Labels you set carry over to the next clip you save, so a run of promos is labelled once.

    These are separate from Tags in search results — those come from the links in your show notes and are the same for everyone. Labels are yours.

    The list

    Press ▶ to hear a clip without opening the editor; a scrubber appears on that row while it plays, and you can click along it to move within the clip. Click a title to rename it. The download arrow saves that one clip as an MP3; ⋯ holds Edit in editor and Remove.

    Removing something gives you ten seconds to undo it. Clips are grouped by when you saved them — today, yesterday, last week.

    Downloading them all

    Download all packs every clip into one zip. You can name the file and add a note — the note is saved inside as clips.txt, alongside a list of every clip with its show, date, times and labels, so the bag explains itself later.

    If you've filtered by a label, the button says Download these 3 rather than Download all, and the file is named …-filtered. You always know which you're getting.

    Where clips are kept

    In this browser, on this computer, and nowhere else. Nothing is uploaded, so the list works offline — but it doesn't follow you to another machine, and clearing your browsing data removes it. The audio itself is never at risk; only the record of what you found.

    A downloaded MP3 is a normal file and outlives all of this.

    Sharing & export

    Sharing what you found

    The address bar always reflects what you're looking at, so any search can be copied and sent to someone — filters, sort order and all. A link can also point at a single moment, opening the editor on it.

    Export

    Export turns the archive into a spreadsheet. One row per episode, with dates a spreadsheet understands, durations as numbers you can total, and links back to the episode, its transcript and its audio.

    • Choose how much. This search gives you exactly what's on screen — the same query, filters and sort — so a filtered view exports as a filtered file. Everything ignores the filters and gives you the whole archive. Both counts are shown, so you always know which file you're about to get.
    • Choose the shape. CSV opens in Sheets, Excel or Numbers. Copy to clipboard skips the file and pastes straight into a sheet you already have open. JSON is for feeding another system. Archive package is all of it at once — see below.
    • See it before you commit. What's in the file, exactly lists every column with a plain description, and shows the first couple of rows of your actual file — in whichever format you've picked.

    Where the rows come from. They're read from this archive, which is built from your two feeds and refreshes daily — nothing is fetched from Podbean when you press the button. That's why it's instant, and why audio and artwork appear as links rather than files: Podbean is still hosting those, and a spreadsheet cell can't hold a 27 MB MP3.

    Transcript text isn't in the spreadsheet, on purpose. One episode runs to about 49,000 characters and a spreadsheet cell stops at 50,000, so it would be cut off without telling you. The links get you the full text instead — and the Transcript button downloads any single episode as plain text or subtitles. The archive package below carries them as real files.

    The archive package

    A single .zip holding the catalogue, the transcripts and a README that explains itself — a copy of the archive that doesn't need this website to be readable. The whole thing, all 200 episodes and every transcript, comes to under 4 MB. It's small enough to email and plain enough that another developer, or any AI, could rebuild the catalogue from it.

    Inside:

    • README.md — what the package is, what every column means, and what to know about the data. Written for someone who has never seen this site.
    • episodes.csv and episodes.json — one row per episode, the same rows as the spreadsheet export.
    • passages.csv — every spoken line with the second it was said, all 14,937 of them. This is the file for finding a quote and knowing exactly where it is in the audio.
    • transcripts/ — one plain-text file per episode, named by date so the folder reads chronologically.

    Transcripts and timestamps are each a tick box, so you can take just the catalogue if that's all you need.

    The filename says what it is. Export with Everything selected and you get …-complete.zip; export a filtered view and you get …-filtered.zip, with a warning inside the README. A partial export is a perfectly good dataset, but it isn't a backup — and a year later nothing else would tell you which one you were looking at.

    Audio isn't in the package, only links to it. The archive's audio runs to 8–12 GB, which is a download rather than a file. Podbean hosts it, and audio_url points straight at each MP3.
